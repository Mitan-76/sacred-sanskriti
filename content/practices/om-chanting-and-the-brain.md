The best-known study on OM chanting is a 2011 brain-scan (fMRI) pilot by Kalyani and colleagues. In 12 healthy volunteers, chanting OM aloud was linked to reduced activity in emotion-related brain regions, including the amygdala. A separate 2022 study of 36 people found that five minutes of chanting increased a heart-rhythm marker of the body's "rest and recover" system. Both studies are small and early, so their results point in a direction rather than settle anything.

OM (also written AUM) is one of the oldest sacred syllables in Hindu tradition. Its spiritual meaning is set out in the Mandukya Upanishad, which we cover in our article on the <a href="/symbols/om-symbol">Om symbol</a>. This article is about something narrower: what researchers have actually measured when people chant it, and what they have not.

## The Studies at a Glance

Two peer-reviewed studies directly measure what happens in the body during OM chanting. Both were published in the *International Journal of Yoga* by teams at India's National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), and both were small. ## What the fMRI Study Found

The 2011 fMRI pilot found that chanting OM aloud lowered activity in a group of emotion-related brain regions, while saying "ssss" did not. Twelve healthy volunteers were scanned in three conditions: chanting OM, pronouncing "ssss," and resting.

Compared with rest, OM chanting showed reduced activity on both sides of the brain in the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, parahippocampal gyri, thalami, and hippocampi, and in the right amygdala, a region involved in fear and threat processing. The "ssss" condition produced neither increases nor decreases in these regions. That comparison matters: it suggests the effect was not simply from making a sustained sound.

The authors noted that similar patterns of deactivation have been recorded with vagus nerve stimulation, a medical treatment used in depression and epilepsy. They concluded the findings "argue for a potential role" of OM chanting in clinical practice. That is a call for more research, not a demonstrated treatment.

## What the Heart Rate Variability Study Found

The 2022 study found that five minutes of loud OM chanting increased high-frequency heart rate variability (HRV), especially in people with yoga experience. HRV is the natural variation in time between heartbeats; its high-frequency component is commonly used as a marker of parasympathetic nervous system activity, the "rest and recover" side of the body's stress system.

Researchers measured 19 experienced yoga practitioners and 17 people new to yoga before and after a single five-minute chanting session. The experienced group started with higher high-frequency power and showed a significantly larger increase after chanting. The size of that increase also tracked with how many years they had practiced. The authors conclude that a brief session "might enhance parasympathetic nervous system activity, promote relaxation, and provide calmness" — careful wording that fits a small, single-session study.

## Does OM Chanting Stimulate the Vagus Nerve?

That idea is still a hypothesis. The vagus nerve links the brain with the heart, lungs, and digestive system and helps shift the body out of a stress response. The Kalyani team proposed that the vibration felt during audible chanting could stimulate the vagus nerve through its branches near the ear. Their study measured brain activity, not vagus nerve activity, so it could not confirm this.

The HRV result is consistent with increased parasympathetic activity, which the vagus nerve helps carry. But neither study tested the vagus nerve directly. Claims that OM chanting "tones the vagus nerve" and so improves digestion, sleep, or immunity go beyond what these studies measured.

## Limitations of the Research

The evidence on OM chanting is early-stage, and three limits apply to everything above.

- **Small samples.** The two studies included 12 and 36 people. Results from groups this small can shift a lot when a study is repeated.
- **Pilot and single-session designs.** Both measured effects during or right after one session. Neither tested whether regular practice over weeks changes anything lasting.
- **Few replications.** We could not find independent studies that repeat these specific findings. Until other teams reproduce them, they are best treated as promising leads.

Broader research on related practices is stronger. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30245619/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2018 systematic review of slow-breathing techniques</a> (15 studies) linked breathing under 10 breaths per minute with higher HRV and reports of greater relaxation. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2014 JAMA Internal Medicine meta-analysis</a> of 47 meditation trials found moderate evidence that mindfulness programs reduce anxiety and depression. OM chanting naturally slows the breath and focuses attention, so these findings make a calming effect plausible. They were not studies of OM chanting itself.

## How to Practice OM Chanting

Chanting OM takes no equipment and a few minutes.

1. **Sit upright.** A chair or the floor is fine. Keep your spine straight, shoulders relaxed, and jaw loose so the breath moves freely.
2. **Inhale** gently and fully through the nose.
3. **Chant in two parts as you exhale.** Start with a long, open "Ooooo," felt in the chest. Then close your lips into "Mmmmm," a hum felt in the face and head. Let the exhale be slow; don't force the sound.
4. **Repeat.** The HRV study used a five-minute session. Many practitioners start with 11 repetitions or about five minutes, then build up as it feels comfortable.

Morning practice can set a calmer tone for the day; evening practice can help you unwind after work. Consistency matters more than the time you pick. ## Why Many People Find OM Chanting Calming

Practitioners often describe OM chanting as calming, and several features of the practice could plausibly contribute. The long exhale slows the breath, which is the mechanism the slow-breathing review found linked to relaxation. The vibration in the chest and face gives the mind a physical sensation to rest on. Focusing on the sound leaves less room for racing thoughts, and steady repetition creates a predictable rhythm.

Not every one of these has been tested in OM chanting specifically. Together they explain why the practice appears in so many traditions of meditation, alongside other sound-based rituals such as <a href="/practices/why-do-we-ring-bells-in-temples">ringing temple bells</a> and reciting the <a href="/mantras-chants/real-meaning-gayatri-mantra">Gayatri Mantra</a>, which itself begins with OM.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What did the OM chanting fMRI study find?

Kalyani and colleagues (2011) scanned 12 healthy volunteers and found that chanting OM aloud reduced activity in emotion-related brain regions, including the amygdala. Saying "ssss" did not have this effect. It was a small pilot study, so the findings are preliminary.

### Does OM chanting reduce stress?

Early research suggests it may support relaxation. A 2022 study found that five minutes of chanting increased a heart-rhythm marker of parasympathetic activity, more so in experienced yoga practitioners. No large trial has yet tested OM chanting as a stress treatment.

### Is OM chanting religious or scientific?

It can be both. In Hindu and yogic traditions, OM is a sacred sound with deep spiritual meaning. Researchers study it as a breathing-and-sound practice with measurable effects on the body. The two views are not in conflict.

### How long should I chant OM each day?

There's no research-based dose yet. The HRV study used a single five-minute session, and many practitioners begin with five minutes or 11 repetitions daily. Regular short practice is a sensible starting point.

### Is OM chanting safe?

For most healthy people, chanting OM is a low-risk practice. If you have a respiratory or heart condition, or any other medical concern, check with a healthcare professional first. It complements medical care but does not replace it.
