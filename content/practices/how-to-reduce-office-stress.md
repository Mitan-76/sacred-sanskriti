Five practices from Hindu and yogic tradition can fit into a workday: *pranayama* (slow, controlled breathing), OM chanting, meditation, short yoga stretches, and simple grounding habits. The evidence behind them differs. Slow breathing and meditation have the strongest research support, OM chanting has small early studies, and desk yoga and grounding have little direct research. Each section below says which is which.

Office stress builds when work demands keep outpacing your ability to recover. Deadlines, back-to-back meetings, constant notifications, and long hours of sitting all add to it. These practices won't remove the pressure, but they are quick, free, and need no equipment, so they're easy to try between meetings or after work.

---

## What Is Office Stress?

Office stress is the mental and physical strain that builds when workplace demands consistently exceed your ability to cope. It isn't one hard day. It's pressure that accumulates week after week without enough recovery.

### Common Causes

- Heavy workload and tight deadlines
- Pressure from managers or performance reviews
- Long hours and poor work-life balance
- Constant emails and notifications
- Job insecurity or unclear expectations
- A difficult workplace culture
- Tiring daily commutes

### Common Signs

- Mental exhaustion and trouble switching off after work
- Overthinking and poor concentration
- Irritability, low motivation, or feeling emotionally flat
- Anxiety, sleep problems, or a racing heartbeat
- Frequent headaches, neck stiffness, or back pain

## How Stress Affects the Brain and Body

Stress triggers the body's fight-or-flight response. When you perceive a threat, even a tense email, stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline rise. Breathing speeds up, muscles tighten, and the brain becomes more alert.

In short bursts, that response is useful. Problems start when it switches on day after day and the body rarely returns to a calm baseline. Long-term stress is widely associated with poorer sleep, burnout, and lower overall well-being. The practices below work on the other side of the system: the parasympathetic, or "rest and recover," response.

## Five Practices That May Help

### 1. Pranayama: Slow, Controlled Breathing

*Pranayama* means deliberately regulating the breath, and it is the best-supported practice in this list. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30245619/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2018 systematic review of 15 studies</a> found that slow breathing (under 10 breaths per minute) in healthy people was linked to higher heart rate variability, a marker of the body's calming response. It was also linked to more relaxation and less anxiety and anger. The review covered slow breathing in general, not these specific traditional techniques.

**Forms that suit office workers:**
- *Anulom Vilom* (alternate-nostril breathing)
- *Bhramari* (humming-bee breath)
- Slow, deep breathing from the belly through the nose

**Good moments to use it:**
- Before an important meeting
- After a difficult call or conversation
- During a lunch break
- Before bed, to wind down

A few minutes is a practical place to start.

**What the evidence shows:** good support for slow breathing in general; little research on the named techniques specifically.

### 2. OM Chanting

OM chanting combines a long exhale, sound vibration, and focused attention. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35444369/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2022 study of 36 adults</a> found that five minutes of loud OM chanting increased a heart-rhythm marker of the body's calming response, more so in experienced yoga practitioners. Our article on <a href="/practices/om-chanting-and-the-brain">OM chanting and the brain</a> sets out this study and an earlier brain-scan pilot side by side, including their limits.

**How to practice:**
1. Sit upright with your eyes closed.
2. Inhale slowly and deeply.
3. As you exhale, chant **"Ooooommmm,"** letting the sound vibrate in your chest.
4. Repeat 5 to 10 times.

**What the evidence shows:** early and promising, but based on small, single-session studies.

### 3. Meditation: Training the Mind to Pause

Meditation doesn't stop thoughts. It trains you to notice them without reacting straight away. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2014 meta-analysis in JAMA Internal Medicine</a> pooled 47 trials with 3,515 participants. It found moderate evidence that mindfulness meditation programs reduce anxiety, depression, and pain, and low evidence that they reduce stress. The same review found insufficient evidence that meditation improves attention or sleep. It found no evidence that meditation works better than other active treatments such as exercise or therapy.

**For beginners:** Sit quietly for five minutes with your eyes closed. Watch your breath come and go without trying to control it. When your mind wanders, gently bring your attention back to the breath. That return is the practice.

**What the evidence shows:** moderate support for anxiety and depression; weaker support for general stress.

### 4. Yoga for Desk Workers

Sitting for eight or more hours a day builds physical tension, and gentle movement can ease it. Simple poses worth knowing:

- **Mountain Pose** — resets your posture
- **Child's Pose** — eases a tense lower back
- **Cat-Cow Stretch** — loosens the spine after long sitting
- **Seated Spinal Twist** — releases tension through the mid-back

None of these needs a mat or a class. Three or four poses in a quiet corner during a break are enough to start.

**What the evidence shows:** limited for short desk routines like this one. For a yoga sequence that has been measured, see our article on <a href="/practices/surya-namaskar">Surya Namaskar</a>, whose effects on heart rate variability have been compared with cycling.

### 5. Grounding Practices

Traditional Indian practice also includes quieter ways to settle the mind: mindful walking, repeating a mantra, sitting in silence, or gazing at a lamp flame. Stress pulls attention into worry about tomorrow or replaying what went wrong. Grounding practices bring it back to the present.

**What the evidence shows:** little direct research on these specific practices. Treat them as habits to try, not proven techniques.

## A 15-Minute Daily Routine

A short daily routine is easier to sustain than occasional long sessions. This one combines the three best-studied practices:

- **Minutes 1–5:** Slow breathing, such as *Anulom Vilom* or slow nasal breathing
- **Minutes 6–10:** OM chanting, 5 to 10 rounds with your attention on the sound
- **Minutes 11–15:** Silent meditation, observing the breath

You can do it before work, at lunch, or in the evening. A morning session can set a calmer tone for the day; an evening session can help you let go of work before sleep.

## What Does the Science Actually Say?

The evidence differs by practice:

- **Strongest support:** slow, controlled breathing and meditation, each backed by systematic reviews of multiple studies.
- **Early support:** OM chanting, with small studies that point in a promising direction but need larger trials.
- **Limited direct evidence:** short desk-yoga routines and grounding habits.

A practice with less research behind it isn't useless. It just shouldn't be described as proven.

## These Practices Don't Replace Professional Help

If you have panic attacks, severe anxiety, depression, trauma, or ongoing sleep problems, please see a qualified healthcare professional. These practices can support your well-being alongside treatment, but they are not a substitute for it.

## Quick Tips for Busy Professionals

**During the workday:**
- Take a two-minute breathing break every 90 minutes or so
- Relax your shoulders and jaw on purpose; tension builds there unnoticed
- Take a short walk after lunch instead of scrolling your phone
- Mute non-essential notifications during focused work

**After work:**
- Spend at least 10 minutes away from screens before winding down
- Do some light stretching before bed
- Stop checking work email after a fixed time each evening

## A 7-Day Experiment

If you're not sure where to start, try this for one week:

- 5 minutes of slow breathing each morning
- 5 minutes of OM chanting
- 5 minutes of silence before sleep

At the end of the week, honestly note any changes in your sleep, your morning mood, your focus, and how quickly irritation rises on difficult days. Your own record is more useful than any general promise.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I reduce office stress quickly?

Slow breathing is the fastest option with research behind it. A few minutes of slow, deep nasal breathing, a glass of water, and a short walk away from your screen are easy to do between tasks. It helps to do this before stress peaks rather than after.

### Which practice is best for beginners?

Slow breathing (*pranayama*) is the most practical place to start. It needs no prior knowledge or equipment, has the strongest research support of the practices here, and can be done almost anywhere, from a parked car to a quiet meeting room.

### How long should I chant OM?

There's no research-based dose yet. The 2022 heart-rate study used a single five-minute session, so five minutes a day is a reasonable start. How closely you attend to the sound matters more than how long you chant.

### Do these practices require religious belief?

No. Slow breathing, focused attention, and rhythmic sound affect the body whatever your beliefs. Many people also value their spiritual meaning, and the two can sit together.

### How soon will I feel results?

It varies. Practitioners often report feeling calmer right after a breathing or chanting session. For longer-term change, the meditation trials in the 2014 review typically measured results after programs of about eight weeks.
