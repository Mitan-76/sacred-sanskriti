Lord Hanuman is one of the most beloved and widely worshipped deities in Hinduism. Known as a divine vanara — a monkey-like celestial being — he is the devoted companion of Lord Rama, celebrated across centuries for his extraordinary strength, fierce loyalty, and complete surrender to the divine.

Yet behind the familiar image of the muscular warrior with a mace lies a figure of remarkable depth — a philosopher, a poet, a grammarian, and above all, the archetype of selfless devotion.

This article answers the most commonly asked questions about Hanuman, drawing from the Ramayana, the Puranas, and regional traditions.

---

## Who is Lord Hanuman?

Lord Hanuman is a central figure in Hinduism, revered as a divine vanara and the most devoted companion of Lord Rama. He is a key character in the ancient epic the Ramayana, where his loyalty, strength, and wisdom shape the course of events from Lanka to Ayodhya.

He is known by many names — **Maruti** (son of Marut, the wind), **Bajrangabali** (one with a body of lightning), **Anjaneya** (son of Anjana), and **Pawanputra** (son of the wind god Vayu).

Hanuman's origins trace to his mother **Anjana**, a celestial apsara, and his divine father **Vayu**, the god of wind. In Vaishnava traditions he is considered Vayu's spiritual son; in many Shaiva traditions he is regarded as a partial incarnation of Shiva himself. His stories appear not only in the Ramayana but also throughout the Mahabharata and various Puranas.

### The Story of His Name

As a child, Hanuman displayed extraordinary power and mischievousness. One morning, feeling hungry, he spotted the rising sun and mistook it for a ripe fruit — and leapt into the sky to eat it. To stop him, Indra, the king of the gods, hurled his thunderbolt, striking Hanuman on the chin and sending him crashing to earth seemingly lifeless. The word *hanu* means jaw in Sanskrit — and this childhood incident is believed to be the origin of the name Hanuman, meaning "one with a broken jaw."

His father Vayu was devastated. In grief, he withdrew from the world — and without air, all living beings began to suffocate. Lord Shiva intervened, revived the child, and Vayu returned. Grateful, the gods assembled and each bestowed blessings upon the young Hanuman: Brahma granted him the ability to change his size at will, Agni made him immune to fire, and Indra — who had struck him — granted him strength equal to his own.

## Is Lord Hanuman Still Alive?

Yes — in Hindu tradition, Lord Hanuman is considered a **chiranjivi**, meaning an immortal who lives through all ages of the world.

His immortality was granted by Sita herself after the events of the Ramayana. Moved by his boundless devotion and service, she declared that Hanuman would enjoy eternal life, free from pain and grief, and that his glory would endure as long as the sun and moon illuminate the universe. She also proclaimed that wherever the name of Rama is recited, Hanuman would be present.

Rama further affirmed this blessing. Even after Hanuman's act of tearing open his chest — which left a wound — Rama healed him with his own touch and confirmed his immortality, despite Hanuman's characteristic humility in asking only for a place at Rama and Sita's feet.

## How Did Hanuman Die?

He did not — at least not in any account recognised across the major Hindu traditions.

While he experienced what appeared to be death as a child after Indra's thunderbolt, Lord Shiva revived him immediately. Beyond that childhood episode, the scriptures contain no account of Hanuman's death. He is one of eight chiranjivis in Hindu tradition — beings blessed to live until the end of the current cosmic cycle, the Kali Yuga.

## Why Did He Tear His Chest?

This is perhaps the most iconic story associated with Hanuman, and it speaks directly to the nature of his devotion.

After Rama's victory over Ravana and his return to Ayodhya, gifts were distributed to all who had aided in the war. When Rama offered Hanuman a gift — specifically a pearl necklace given by Sita — Hanuman accepted it, examined each pearl carefully, and then discarded them. The court was bewildered. Officials questioned him: what was he looking for?

Hanuman replied that he was searching for Rama's name inside each pearl. Since none contained it, they were worthless to him.

Lakshmana, unconvinced, challenged him — pointing out that Rama's name did not appear on Hanuman's body either. Without a word, Hanuman tore open his chest with his own nails. Inside, on every bone and across his heart, the name of Rama was inscribed — thousands upon thousands of times. Sita and Rama were visible within his very being.

Rama immediately healed the wound with his own touch. The court fell silent.

This story is not merely dramatic — it is considered the definitive illustration of *bhakti* in its highest form: devotion so complete that the beloved lives within the devotee. In the framework of [Dharma](/hinduism-qa/what-is-dharma-in-hinduism), this total surrender to one's highest purpose is the ultimate expression of svadharma — one's own sacred duty, lived without reservation.

## Why Do We Apply Sindoor to Lord Hanuman?

The tradition of applying sindoor — red vermilion — to Hanuman's idol has its roots in a simple and touching story from the Ramayana.

Hanuman once observed Sita applying sindoor to the parting of her hair. Curious, he asked her why. Sita explained that the sindoor was a symbol of her love for Rama and her prayer for his long life and well-being. A small mark on her forehead, she said, brought him health and protection.

Hanuman, characteristically literal in his boundless devotion, reasoned: if a small amount brought Rama such benefit, then covering his entire body in sindoor would surely protect Rama far more completely. He proceeded to smear himself head to toe in vermilion.

Rama, amused and deeply moved, declared that any devotee who offered sindoor to Hanuman would receive his blessings — strength, courage, and protection. The tradition has been carried forward ever since.

## Was Hanuman Married?

The mainstream Ramayana and most major Hindu texts depict Hanuman as a lifelong **Brahmachari** — a celibate devoted entirely to the service of Rama. This is the most widely accepted and revered aspect of his character.

However, regional traditions — particularly in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh — hold that Hanuman was briefly and symbolically married to **Suvarchala**, the daughter of Surya, the sun god.

According to this tradition, Hanuman wished to learn certain sacred knowledge that only Surya could teach. Surya stipulated that he would only impart this knowledge to a married man. To fulfil the condition and complete his education, Hanuman entered a symbolic marriage with Suvarchala — with the understanding that it involved no physical union and did not compromise his vow of celibacy. Suvarchala is said to have returned to her father after the marriage, leaving Hanuman to continue his celibate life of devotion.

This story is not universally accepted, and the predominant image of Hanuman across most of India remains that of the eternal celibate and bachelor ascetic.

## How Is He So Powerful?

Hanuman's power is understood through multiple dimensions — divine lineage, blessings received, and the extraordinary amplifying force of his devotion.

- **Divine parentage** — As the son of Vayu, the wind god, Hanuman inherited incredible speed, the ability to fly, and elemental strength beyond human reckoning
- **Blessings at birth** — After the thunderbolt incident, the assembled gods each granted him powers: Indra gave him strength, Brahma gave him the ability to change size, Agni made him fireproof, Varuna protected him from water
- **Forgotten and remembered** — As a child, Hanuman's mischievous nature led powerful sages to place a curse on him, causing him to forget his divine powers until reminded. It was Jambavan, the elder bear king, who reminded Hanuman of his latent abilities when the army needed someone to leap across the ocean to Lanka
- **Devotion as power** — Perhaps most significantly, the tradition holds that Hanuman's devotion to Rama is itself a source of power — that *bhakti* is not separate from strength but is its highest expression

His feats in the Ramayana reflect all of this: leaping across the ocean to Lanka in a single bound, burning Lanka with his own tail after Ravana's soldiers set it alight, carrying an entire Himalayan mountain peak back to the battlefield to find the Sanjeevani herb that would save Lakshmana's life. The shore from which he leapt — Rameswaram — is today the site of the [Ramanathaswamy Jyotirlinga](/hinduism-qa/12-jyotirlinga-complete-guide), one of the 12 most sacred Shiva shrines in India, installed there by Lord Rama himself after the war.

## Why Is the Hanuman Chalisa So Powerful?

The **Hanuman Chalisa** is a devotional hymn of forty verses composed in Awadhi by the poet-saint Tulsidas in the 16th century. It is among the most recited prayers in the Hindu world — chanted daily by tens of millions of people across traditions, regions, and languages.

Its power is attributed to several interlocking reasons:

- **Concentrated praise** — Each of the forty verses captures a specific quality, deed, or aspect of Hanuman. Reciting it is a structured meditation on his complete character
- **Sita's blessing** — Sita declared that Hanuman would be present wherever Rama's name is recited. The Chalisa, by praising Hanuman's devotion to Rama, is believed to invoke his active presence
- **Protection** — The Chalisa is widely recited for protection from fear, illness, negative forces, and obstacles. This stems from Hanuman's own role as a protector — the one who guarded Rama, crossed impossible distances, and subdued powerful demons
- **Accessibility** — Its rhythmic, melodic structure makes it easy to memorise and recite daily. Tulsidas wrote it in the vernacular, not Sanskrit, deliberately making it available to all — regardless of caste, education, or background
- **Tradition of results** — Centuries of lived testimony across India give the Chalisa a cultural and spiritual weight that amplifies the faith of each new practitioner

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Hanuman a God or a monkey?

Hanuman is revered as a deity in Hinduism, depicted as a divine vanara — a celestial monkey-like being. He is considered the spiritual son of Vayu, the wind god, and in some traditions an incarnation of Shiva himself.

### What is Hanuman's greatest power?

Hanuman possesses many powers — immense strength, flight, and the ability to change his size at will. But the tradition consistently holds that his greatest power is his devotion to Rama, which is the source and sustainer of all his other abilities.

### What is the significance of Hanuman's broken jaw?

It is believed to be the origin of his name. As a child he leapt to eat the sun, Indra struck him with a thunderbolt, and the blow broke his jaw. The Sanskrit word *hanu* means jaw — hence Hanuman.

### Why is Hanuman considered celibate?

His celibacy is an expression of complete devotion — all his energy, undivided, directed toward the service of Rama. In the tradition, Brahmacharya (celibacy) is not merely physical restraint but a total orientation of one's being toward the divine.

### What is the Hanuman Chalisa?

A devotional hymn of forty verses composed by Tulsidas in the 16th century. It praises Hanuman's qualities and deeds, and is widely recited for courage, protection, and spiritual strength.

### Did Hanuman have children?

Some regional traditions mention a son named **Makardhwaja**, said to have been born from a drop of Hanuman's sweat that fell into the ocean during his leap to Lanka and was swallowed by a great fish. This account is not universally accepted and is absent from the mainstream Ramayana text.

### What is Hanuman's role in the Ramayana?

He is the crucial link between Rama and Sita — the one who crossed the ocean, found her in Lanka, brought back proof of her survival, helped build the bridge, fought in the war, and flew through the night to fetch the herb that saved Lakshmana's life. Without Hanuman, the Ramayana's resolution is impossible.
