The Mahabharat is one of the longest and most profound epic poems ever written. At its centre is the Kurukshetra War — a dynastic struggle between two groups of cousins, the Pandavas and the Kauravas, for the throne of Hastinapura.

But the Mahabharat is far more than a war story. It is an exploration of dharma, duty, sacrifice, and the moral complexity of human nature — questions as relevant today as they were when the epic was first composed.

This article answers the most commonly asked questions about the Mahabharat, drawing from the epic itself and scholarly historical analysis.

## Where Was the Mahabharat War Fought?

The Mahabharat War was fought in Kurukshetra, a region in north India in present-day Haryana. The epic names this site *Kurukshetra* — literally "the field of the Kurus" — and also calls it *Dharmakshetra*, meaning "the field of dharma."

The text suggests this location was chosen for its sanctity, with the implication that acts committed there would be answered by dharma. Despite the war spanning only eighteen days, the battle narrative constitutes over a quarter of the entire Mahabharata — a measure of its centrality to the epic.

## Who Fought on the Side of the Pandavas?

The five Pandava brothers — Yudhishthir, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva — were the central combatants on their side. Their most vital ally was Krishna, who served as Arjuna's charioteer and provided divine counsel throughout the war.

Allied kingdoms on the Pandava side included the Panchalas, the Matsyas, the Chedis, and the Yadavas, among others. The combined Pandava army comprised 7 *akshauhinis* — a large ancient military formation encompassing infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants. The Kaurava army was larger, fielding 11 *akshauhinis*.

## Who Was the Real Villain of the Mahabharat?

Duryodhana, the eldest of the hundred Kaurava brothers, is widely regarded as the primary villain. His insatiable greed, envy, and refusal to grant the Pandavas their rightful share of the kingdom were the root causes of the war.

His actions — driven by arrogance and an absolute desire for power — led to repeated injustices against the Pandavas, culminating in the Kurukshetra War. While other characters made morally questionable decisions, Duryodhana's unwavering malice is consistently portrayed as the point from which the conflict becomes irresolvable.

## The Story of Eklavya and Dronacharya

The story of Eklavya is one of the most morally striking in the entire epic, found in the Adi Parva — the first book of the Mahabharata.

Eklavya, the son of Hiranyadhanus — a Nishada king — approached Dronacharya, the royal preceptor of the Kuru princes, and asked to be taught archery. Dronacharya refused, citing Eklavya's low social standing and his concern that the boy's ability might one day surpass that of his favoured student, Arjuna.

Undeterred, Eklavya returned to the forest, fashioned a clay idol of Dronacharya, and taught himself through relentless practice — treating the idol as his guru. He became an extraordinarily skilled archer.

During a hunting expedition, a dog belonging to the Pandavas encountered Eklavya and began to bark. Without harming the animal, Eklavya shot seven arrows simultaneously into its mouth — a feat of precision that astonished Arjuna when he witnessed it.

When it emerged that Eklavya considered Dronacharya his teacher, Arjuna felt threatened — Dronacharya had promised him he would be the greatest archer of his time. Dronacharya then approached Eklavya and demanded his right thumb as *gurudakshina* — the traditional offering to one's teacher. Eklavya, out of reverence and devotion, severed his thumb willingly.

The story raises questions the epic does not resolve easily: about caste, loyalty, the obligations of a teacher, and whether sacrifice made out of devotion can also be unjust.

## How Many Sons Did Dhritarashtra and Gandhari Have?

According to the Mahabharat, Dhritarashtra and Gandhari had one hundred sons — collectively known as the Kauravas — and one daughter, Duhsala. Duryodhana was their eldest, followed by Duhshasana and ninety-eight others.

This vast lineage and their alliances gave the Kaurava army its numerical superiority on the battlefield.

## Was the Mahabharat War a Real Historical Event?

This remains one of the most debated questions in the study of the epic.

While the Mahabharata is a poem — not a historical chronicle — many scholars believe it preserves the memory of a real conflict. Historian A. L. Basham considered the popular tradition placing the war in 3102 BCE historically impossible, and suggested a more credible estimate of around the beginning of the 9th century BCE, broadly consistent with archaeological findings from the early Iron Age and with the dating of Brahmana literature.

Archaeological work at Hastinapura, the capital associated with the Kuru clan in the epic, has revealed cultural layers from the early Iron Age — though no direct evidence has been found linking specific figures or events to the poem's narrative.

Most scholars treat the Mahabharata as a work that blends historical memory, mythology, and philosophical teaching — not a literal account, but not pure invention either.

## Why Did the Mahabharat War Happen?

The war grew from a dynastic succession dispute, but several specific events made it inevitable:

- **Duryodhana's refusal** — He would not cede any portion of the kingdom to the Pandavas, despite their rightful claim to a share of Hastinapura's throne
- **The dice game** — In a rigged gambling match, Yudhishthir lost his kingdom, his brothers, and Draupadi herself to Duryodhana — a violation of every principle of dharma
- **The exile and its aftermath** — After thirteen years of exile, the Pandavas requested even five villages in return. Duryodhana famously declared he would not give them enough land to stick a needle
- **The violation of dharma** — The Kauravas' treatment of Draupadi in the royal court created a moral rupture that diplomacy could not repair

Krishna made one final peace attempt before the battle began. It was refused. The war that followed lasted eighteen days and ended with nearly total destruction on both sides.

## Who Were the Greatest Warriors in the Mahabharat?

The epic features warriors of extraordinary ability. Among the most prominent:

- **Arjuna** — The third Pandava, considered the finest archer of his age, wielding the divine Gandiva bow under Krishna's guidance
- **Karna** — Eldest son of Kunti and half-brother to the Pandavas, who fought for the Kauravas. He possessed divine armour and earrings that rendered him effectively invincible until they were surrendered
- **Bhishma** — Grand-uncle to both sides, a peerless warrior and strategist who held the boon of choosing the moment of his own death
- **Dronacharya** — Royal preceptor and master of archery and military science, teacher of both the Pandavas and Kauravas
- **Bhima** — The second Pandava, renowned for immense physical strength and mastery of the mace
- **Krishna** — Primarily an advisor and charioteer, but whose strategic intelligence and divine intervention proved decisive in the war's outcome

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many days did the Mahabharat War last?

The Kurukshetra War lasted eighteen days.

### Who won the Mahabharat War?

The Pandavas won, but at enormous cost — nearly everyone they held dear was lost in the conflict.

### What is the main theme of the Mahabharat?

The epic's central themes are dharma, duty, justice, and the consequences of war — set against a dynastic struggle that pulls every character toward impossible choices.

### Who wrote the Mahabharat?

According to tradition, the Mahabharata was composed by Krishna-Dwaipayan Vyasa, who also appears as a character within the epic. He is said to have dictated the verses while Ganesha transcribed them.

### What is the significance of the Bhagavad Gita in the Mahabharat?

The Bhagavad Gita forms part of the Mahabharat — a dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna on the eve of battle. It addresses duty, the self, action without attachment, and the relationship between the individual and the divine. It has become one of the most widely studied philosophical texts in the world.

### How many verses are in the Mahabharat?

The Mahabharata is generally considered to contain around 100,000 verses, making it the longest epic poem ever written.
