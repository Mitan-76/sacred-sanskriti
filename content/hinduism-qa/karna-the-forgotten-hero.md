Karna is one of the most compelling and tragic figures in the [Mahabharata](/hinduism-qa/mahabharat-facts-you-should-know) — a warrior of extraordinary skill, born of divine parentage, raised in hardship, and bound by a loyalty that ultimately cost him everything. He fought on the losing side of the Kurukshetra War not out of ignorance, but with full knowledge of who he was and what it would mean.

Known also as Vasusena, Radheya, Sutaputra, and Anga-Raja, Karna's story is not simply that of a great warrior who died in battle. It is an exploration of identity, gratitude, and the weight of choices made when no path is clean.

This article answers the most commonly asked questions about Karna, drawing from the Mahabharata text and its commentaries.

---

## Who Was Karna?

Karna was the firstborn son of Princess Kunti — making him the eldest of all the Pandavas, though he never fought alongside them. Before she married King Pandu, Kunti had received a boon from the sage Durvasa that allowed her to invoke any deity and bear a child. Testing it out of curiosity, she called upon Surya, the sun god, and Karna was born — radiant, already wearing divine armour and earrings that rendered him invincible.

Fearing disgrace over her unmarried motherhood, Kunti placed the infant in a basket and set him adrift on the river. He was found and raised by Radha and Adhiratha, a charioteer in the court of King Dhritarashtra. This act of abandonment — and the life it set in motion — forms the foundation of Karna's entire tragedy.

---

## Why Was Karna So Loyal to Duryodhana?

Karna's loyalty to Duryodhana was built on gratitude, and gratitude was something Karna treated as more binding than blood.

From his youth, Karna was repeatedly humiliated. At Drona's tournament — where the Pandava princes displayed their martial skills — Karna arrived and challenged Arjuna. Before they could duel, he was disqualified: the contest was for *kshatriyas* (warriors of noble birth), and Karna, raised by a charioteer, was considered a *sutaputra* regardless of his abilities. He was turned away publicly.

It was Duryodhana who stepped forward that day. He immediately crowned Karna the King of Anga, making him a king in his own right and thus eligible to compete. For Karna, this was not a small gesture — it was the first time anyone of rank had recognised him without prejudice.

That bond held for thirteen years. When Krishna later revealed Karna's true identity as the eldest Pandava and offered him the throne of Indraprastha if he switched sides, Karna refused. He stated plainly that it was the bonds built through time and loyalty — not blood — that mattered. He would not abandon the friend who had stood by him when no one else would.

## Who Were Karna's Real Parents?

Karna's biological parents were Surya, the sun deity, and Princess Kunti — the same Kunti who was mother to Yudhishthir, Bhima, and Arjuna.

However, Karna consistently identified Radha and Adhiratha as his true parents. He stated that Radha gave him love and nourishment, and Adhiratha guided him into adult life. When Kunti finally came to him before the war to reveal the truth and ask him to join the Pandavas, Karna acknowledged his birth — but did not change his position. He told her he would spare the lives of all her other sons, so that she would still have five sons when the war ended, whichever side won.

## Why Was Karna Called Danveer Karna?

*Danveer* means "hero of giving" — and the title was earned through a commitment to charity so absolute that it became the defining feature of his public life.

Karna had taken a vow never to refuse anyone who approached him with a request, particularly during his morning prayers to Surya. His reputation was so established that even his enemies acknowledged it. When Indra, father of Arjuna, came disguised as a Brahmin and asked for Karna's divine armour and earrings — the very things that made him invincible — Karna gave them away. He knew it was likely a trick. He gave them anyway.

Surya himself had warned Karna beforehand. Karna's response was that his reputation for generosity was his truest possession, and he would not preserve his body at the cost of his character.

## Why Did Krishna Want Karna to Join the Pandavas?

Krishna approached Karna before the war with a striking offer: join the Pandavas, be recognised as their eldest brother, marry Draupadi, and rule as king. He knew that Karna's presence on the Kaurava side was one of the greatest strategic threats the Pandavas faced, and that Karna's defection would potentially end the conflict before a single battle was fought.

Karna refused. He told Krishna that he could not undo thirteen years of friendship for a throne. He also pointed out that if he switched sides now, Duryodhana would be left without his most capable ally — and that abandoning him at the moment of greatest danger would be a form of treachery he could not commit, whatever the personal gain.

## Was Karna Stronger Than Arjuna?

The Mahabharata itself does not offer a simple answer — and it is worth noting that it does not need to.

Duryodhana considered Karna the equal of Arjuna, and the epic supports this view in most contexts. Karna was born with divine armour, trained under Parashurama — one of the greatest masters of arms in the tradition — and possessed several celestial weapons including the *Vasava Shakti*, an irresistible weapon given to him by Indra.

However, Karna fought the Kurukshetra War stripped of several advantages. He had given away his natural armour to Indra. Parashurama had cursed him to forget critical knowledge of weapons at the moment he needed it most. A Brahmin whose cow Karna had accidentally killed also cursed him — that his chariot wheel would sink into the earth at a decisive moment.

When Karna and Arjuna finally met in their duel on the seventeenth day, all of these converged. The wheel sank. Karna stepped down to free it — unarmed. In that moment, guided by Krishna, Arjuna loosed the arrow that killed him.

Whether Karna was the stronger warrior in absolute terms remains open. What is clear is that he never got to fight the duel his abilities deserved.

## How Did Karna Die?

Karna died on the seventeenth day of the Kurukshetra War, in his long-awaited duel with Arjuna.

During the battle, the Brahmin's curse took effect — Karna's chariot wheel sank into the soft ground and became lodged in the earth. Karna called out to Arjuna to pause, invoking the warrior's code that prohibits attacking an unarmed man who cannot fight. Krishna, riding as Arjuna's charioteer, reminded Arjuna of every violation of that same code the Kauravas had committed — the dice game, the disrobing of Draupadi, the killing of Abhimanyu. Arjuna released the arrow. Karna died trying to free his wheel.

## Why Was Karna Cursed Multiple Times?

Three significant curses shaped Karna's fate:

- **Parashurama's curse** — Parashurama taught only Brahmins. Karna presented himself as one to gain instruction. During a rest, a larva burrowed into Karna's leg. He bore the pain silently rather than disturb his guru. When Parashurama woke and saw the wound, he understood that no Brahmin could endure such pain without crying out — only a warrior trained to suppress pain could do this. He cursed Karna to forget the knowledge of his most powerful weapons at the moment of greatest need
- **The Brahmin's curse** — Karna accidentally killed a Brahmin's cow with an arrow while hunting. The Brahmin cursed him that the earth would swallow his chariot wheel during a battle when his life depended on it
- **The circumstances of his birth** — Not a formal curse, but Kunti's abandonment placed Karna outside the social structure that would otherwise have recognised and protected him. The humiliation he faced his entire life was a direct consequence

## What Were Karna's Biggest Mistakes?

Karna's greatest failing was not disloyalty or cowardice — it was a willingness to follow Duryodhana into territory he knew was wrong.

He was present during the dice game where the Pandavas lost everything and Draupadi was publicly humiliated. He did not intervene. He spoke harshly of Draupadi in that assembly — words he later regretted but could not take back. He accepted honours from Duryodhana while Duryodhana pursued power through increasingly unjust means.

Karna was not blind to this. He frequently advised Duryodhana to fight honourably and pursue peace. But he never withdrew his support. His loyalty — his greatest virtue — was also the mechanism by which he became complicit in injustice. In the framework of [dharma](/hinduism-qa/what-is-dharma-in-hinduism), this is the central tension of his character: a man of genuine nobility who could not find a way to separate his loyalty from his conscience.

## Why Did Kunti Reveal the Truth So Late?

Kunti came to Karna just before the war began — after all peace negotiations had failed.

She had kept the secret most of her life out of fear: fear of disgrace, fear of what her sons would think, fear of what it would mean to acknowledge the son she had set adrift. When she finally came to him, it was not purely out of love — she came hoping he would switch sides, or at least promise to spare her other sons.

Karna's response was measured and, in its way, deeply generous. He acknowledged the truth of his birth. He told her the secret was safe. And he made her a promise: that she would not lose five sons in the war. He would face only Arjuna. He would spare Yudhishthir, Bhima, Nakula, and Sahadeva when he had the opportunity to kill them. One of them would die — either Karna or Arjuna — but she would still have five sons when it was over.

That promise held. Karna kept the word he gave to the mother who had abandoned him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Was Karna aware of his true parentage throughout his life?

No. Karna grew up believing Radha and Adhiratha were his parents. He learned the truth from Kunti shortly before the Kurukshetra War. He did not let the revelation change his allegiances.

### What was Karna's relationship with the Pandavas?

Biologically, Karna was the eldest of all the Pandavas — older than Yudhishthir. But he spent his life on the opposing side, bound to Duryodhana and the Kauravas. His relationship with Arjuna was one of long-standing rivalry, shaped by humiliations from their youth and the knowledge that they were the two greatest archers of their generation.

### Did Karna ever regret his loyalty to Duryodhana?

He regretted specific acts — particularly his conduct during Draupadi's humiliation. But he did not regret the loyalty itself. He maintained to the end that Duryodhana had given him his dignity when the world had stripped it away, and that no offer — not even a throne — could justify abandoning that.

### What made Karna a tragic hero?

His tragedy is that his finest qualities — loyalty, generosity, self-discipline — operated in a context that turned them against him. His generosity stripped him of his protection. His loyalty bound him to injustice. His birth excluded him from recognition his abilities warranted. He was not defeated by weakness. He was defeated by the full expression of his virtues in a situation that could not reward them.

### What was the significance of Karna's armour and earrings?

Karna was born with the *Kavacha* (divine armour) and *Kundala* (earrings) fused to his body — gifts from Surya that made him effectively invincible in battle. He gave them to Indra when the god came disguised as a Brahmin and asked for them during Karna's morning prayers. Karna knew the request was likely a trap. He gave them anyway, because refusing would have violated his vow. In exchange, a guilt-stricken Indra gave him the *Vasava Shakti* — a weapon capable of killing any one enemy.

### Did Karna have children?

Yes. Karna was married and had sons. When Krishna came with the offer to switch sides, Karna mentioned his sons and grandsons as part of the life he had built — reinforcing that he considered Radha and Adhiratha's family his own, not the Pandava lineage he was being offered.

### What was Karna's role in the disrobing of Draupadi?

Karna was present in the assembly and did not intervene. He also spoke harshly about Draupadi during the episode. It is widely regarded as the most significant moral failure of his life — a moment where his loyalty to Duryodhana overrode his own sense of what was right.
