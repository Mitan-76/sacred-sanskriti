"33 koti devtas" is one of Hinduism's most widely repeated — and most widely mistranslated — numbers. It is popularly rendered as "33 crore (330 million) gods," but the Sanskrit word *koti* (कोटि) carries two distinct meanings, and the Vedic texts that use this phrase are almost certainly using the second one: "class" or "category," not the numeral "ten million."

This is a textbook case of the gap between popular belief and what the texts actually say — the same kind of gap explored in our piece on <a href="/myth-vs-text/kali-yuga-everything-you-need-to-know">Kali Yuga</a> — and resolving it changes the claim from an impossibly large, unspecified number of deities into a short, nameable, and well-documented list of 33.

---

## What Does "33 Koti Devta" Actually Mean?

The phrase means 33 *categories* of Vedic deities, not 33 crore individual gods. The confusion comes entirely from the word *koti*, which the standard Sanskrit dictionaries (Monier-Williams and Apte) record with two unrelated senses: "ten million, a crore" in its numerical use, and "a class, department, or kingdom" in its categorical use — confirmed directly in the <a href="https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/koti" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">standard Sanskrit dictionary entry for the word</a>. When the Vedic Samhitas speak of 33 *koti* devas, the grammar and context point to the categorical sense: thirty-three types of deity, not thirty-three crore individuals.

## Who Are the 33 Devas?

The Vedic Samhitas name the 33 as 12 Adityas, 11 Rudras, 8 Vasus, and 2 Ashvins — a breakdown confirmed in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikipedia's summary of the Vedic classification of devas</a>:

- **12 Adityas** — solar deities, one presiding over each month of the year, with Indra and Vishnu counted among their number in later tradition
- **11 Rudras** — storm and fierce aspects of Shiva, associated with the vital life-force
- **8 Vasus** — deities of material elements and abundance (including fire, wind, and earth)
- **2 Ashvins** — twin horsemen gods associated with medicine, health, and the morning and evening twilight

This is a complete, specific, nameable list — the opposite of the vague "330 million gods" framing the mistranslation produces.

## Where Does the "33 Crore" Misreading Come From?

The misreading comes from collapsing *koti*'s two meanings into one. Because "crore" is by far the more common modern usage of the word in everyday Hindi and other Indic languages, it is the meaning most people reach for by default, even in a context — ancient Vedic enumeration of deity-classes — where the older, categorical sense is clearly intended. The error is compounded over centuries of oral retelling, where "33 types of gods" gradually flattens into the catchier, more dramatic-sounding "33 crore gods."

## Is There Textual Evidence for a Larger Number of Gods Elsewhere?

Yes, and it argues against the "330 million" figure even more directly. In the <a href="https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-brihadaranyaka-upanishad/d/doc118360.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (3.9)</a>, the sage Yajnavalkya is asked how many gods there are, and gives a chain of answers that steps *down* — not up — from "three thousand and three," through thirty-three, then six, three, two, one and a half, and finally **one**, which he identifies with the vital force (*prana*) and Brahman itself. At each step he clarifies that the larger numbers are restatements of the smaller ones, not additional gods.

Thirty-three appears early in that descent, and the Upanishad's own breakdown of it — eight Vasus, eleven Rudras, twelve Adityas, plus Indra and Prajapati — differs slightly from the Vedic Samhitas' list above, which pairs the Adityas, Rudras, and Vasus with the two Ashvins instead. Both versions agree on the count of 33 and its categorical structure; they differ only on which two deities complete the final pair, a known variation across Vedic sources. Either way, the textual trajectory moves toward fewer, not more, named entities — the opposite direction from "330 million."

## Why Does This Distinction Matter?

Because the two readings describe genuinely different religious concepts: a finite, structured pantheon of 33 named functional categories versus an unbounded, unspecified multitude. Treating "33 crore" as literal also obscures the actual architecture of Vedic theology, which organizes deities by function and domain (solar, storm, elemental, twin) rather than by sheer accumulated number. Getting the translation right restores the text's own, more precise claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Hinduism really have 33 crore (330 million) gods?

No. The "33 crore" figure comes from mistranslating *koti*, a Sanskrit word that means both "crore" and "category." In the Vedic context, it means 33 categories of deities, not 330 million individual gods.

### Who are the 33 Vedic devas?

12 Adityas, 11 Rudras, 8 Vasus, and 2 Ashvins — a specific, nameable list of deity-categories recorded in the Vedic Samhitas.

### Where does the phrase "33 koti devta" come from?

It appears in the Vedic Samhitas' enumeration of deities, and is also connected to a passage in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad where the sage Yajnavalkya walks through a descending chain of answers — 3,003, then 33, then 6, 3, 2, 1½, and finally one — about how many gods there are.

### Why do people say there are 330 million Hindu gods?

Because *koti* is far more commonly used today to mean "crore" (ten million) than its older sense of "category," so the phrase was popularly reinterpreted as a literal, enormous number over centuries of retelling.
